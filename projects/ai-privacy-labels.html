Project 008 / 010

AI Privacy Labels

Do "nutrition labels" for AI actually help people understand what happens to their data? In a 62-person experiment, a short, guarantee-focused label beat a dense technical one on understanding and confidence. Neither label changed whether people would use the product.

Year
2025
Type
Online experiment
With
CMU 17-331 · Prof. Hana Habib
Report
PDF, 15 pages
Read the report ↗ Download PDF ↓

01Question

Companies like Twilio and Motorola Solutions publish "AI nutrition labels" that summarize which models a product uses, what data it keeps, whether that data trains future models, and how much human oversight there is. Most research asks whether to label AI at all, not how a label's design changes what people take away from it.

We asked two things: how label design affects people's understanding of an AI system's data practices and their confidence in that understanding, and whether it changes their willingness to use the system.

02Method

  1. 01Design

    Two labels for a fictional assistant, "SpecAI": a dense Twilio-style table and a short Motorola-style card

  2. 02Recruit

    62 US adults on Prolific who use AI tools, each shown one label at random

  3. 03Measure

    Likert ratings of understanding, confidence and willingness, plus open-ended feedback

  4. 04Analyze

    Descriptive comparisons between conditions and a thematic analysis of comments

  • Survey experiment

  • Shorter labels are understood better

    The concise, guarantee-focused label scored higher on perceived understanding (3.5 vs 2.9) and confidence (3.0 vs 2.3), although both labels described the same system.

  • Understanding didn't change adoption

    Willingness to use was the same under both labels (2.4 of 5). Labels work as education, not as a lever on adoption.

  • Reluctant users already use it

    Almost everyone who said they wouldn't use SpecAI already uses ChatGPT regularly, which is the kind of system the labels actually described.

  • A knowledge gap

    Participants with AI/ML background felt more in control after reading a label; those without it felt less.

  • What people wanted

    Plain-language definitions, and clear answers on retention periods, training-data use and human oversight.

04Recommendations

  • Label designers

    Lead with a few concrete guarantees in plain language rather than disclosing everything. Define terms like "guardrails" and "human in the loop".

  • Policymakers

    Standardize a concise label format, as nutrition labels did, and make retention and training-data use the most visible fields.

  • Anyone mandating a label

    Test it with a diverse population, and show it where decisions are made instead of burying it in settings.

05Limitations & ethics

The two labels differed in length, depth, layout and framing all at once, so no single feature explains the effect. Understanding was self-reported rather than tested, the product was hypothetical, and 62 English-speaking Prolific participants don't represent all AI users.

Participants gave informed consent and were paid for their time. Low-quality responses were excluded before analysis. The study collected no identifying information, and this page doesn't reproduce any participant comments.

06Report

The full report, Do AI Privacy Labels Help People Better Understand How Their Data Is Used?, includes both labels, every chart and the policy implications.
